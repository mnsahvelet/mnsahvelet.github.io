When I first met the **Goda formula** for wave forces on a vertical breakwater, it looked
like an intimidating pile of coefficients — `α₁`, `α₂`, `α₃`, `η*`, `p₁`, `p₃`, `p_u`. Then
someone showed me the *picture*, and it all clicked. The whole method is really just one
trapezoid and one triangle.

## The trapezoid on the front face

The horizontal wave pressure is largest at the still-water line — that's **p₁**:

$$p_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos\beta)(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2\cos^2\beta)\,\rho g H_D.$$

The three `α`'s are just physical "dials":

- **α₁** grows with wave period — long waves press harder.
- **α₂** is the mound effect — a high rubble foundation forces the wave to break onto the
  wall and spikes the pressure.
- **α₃** sets how much of `p₁` survives down to the base (**p₃ = α₃·p₁**).

Above the water line the pressure tapers to zero at the run-up height `η* = 0.75(1+cosβ)H_D`.
That's the top of the trapezoid.

## The triangle underneath

Water also pushes **up** on the base — the uplift `p_u`, maximum at the seaward edge and
zero at the heel. It's small in symbol but deadly in practice, because it directly reduces
the weight holding the caisson down.

## Why the picture is the whole game

Add the trapezoid to get the horizontal force `P`; add the triangle to get the uplift `U`.
Then the two failure checks fall right out:

- **Sliding:** does friction on the reduced weight beat the push? `SF = μ(W−U)/P`.
- **Overturning:** does the weight's moment beat the wave's? `SF = W·B/2 ÷ M`.

That's it. Every caisson I've ever checked came down to keeping those two numbers above
about 1.2. Once you can *see* the trapezoid and the triangle, the coefficients stop being
scary — they're just setting the shape of a picture you already understand.
