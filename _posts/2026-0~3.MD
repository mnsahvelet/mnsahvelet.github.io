One of the first things you learn about ocean waves is that, in deep water, their heights
follow a **Rayleigh distribution**. That gives you a set of neat, fixed ratios — for
example the 2%-exceedance height is always

$$H_{2\%} \approx 1.40\,H_{1/3}.$$

It's a lovely result, and it's wrong exactly where we build most of our structures: **on a
shallow foreshore.**

## Why the tail gets cut off

As waves shoal into shallow water, the biggest ones feel the bottom first and **break**.
The small waves keep going untouched. So the top of the distribution — the very waves we
care about for design — gets shaved off, while the rest stays roughly Rayleigh. The
population is no longer homogeneous.

## The fix: a two-part distribution

Battjes and Groenendijk (2000) handled this with a **composite Weibull distribution**:
a Rayleigh part for the small waves, and a steeper Weibull part (exponent 3.6) for the
large ones, joined at a transition height `H_tr` that depends on the water depth and the
foreshore slope:

$$H_{tr} = (0.35 + 5.8\tan\alpha)\,h.$$

Feed in the local `H_m0` and the depth, read the ratios `H_{1/3}/H_{rms}`, `H_{2\%}/H_{rms}`
off their table, and you get the *real* design heights at the toe. The headline
consequence: in shallow water `H_{2\%}/H_{1/3}` drops well **below 1.40** — sometimes down
to about 1.2. If you'd kept assuming Rayleigh, you'd have over-predicted your extreme
wave.

## Why I care about it

Every downstream calculation — overtopping, armour size, wave forces — starts from the
wave height **at the structure toe**. Get that distribution wrong and everything after it
inherits the error. This is the quiet, unglamorous step that makes the rest of the design
honest.

*There's a small tool that plots the composite distribution and reads off `H_{1/3}`,
`H_{1/10}` and `H_{2\%}` for you
[here](https://mnsahvelet.github.io/coastal-tools/wave-toe-conditions.html).*
